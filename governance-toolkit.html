AI Governance Toolkit for a Transformation Office

Governance that keeps AI moving instead of slowing it down. Low-risk use cases get a fast lane, regulated and high-impact ones get the full set of controls, and leadership gets a portfolio view of value, not just activity. The tools are aligned to the NIST AI Risk Management Framework.

The problems it solves

AI transformation offices tend to hit the same four problems once the number of use cases grows past a handful.

Too many ideas, no consistent way to chooseEvery workstream brings use cases. Without one intake and one scoring method, priority goes to whoever is loudest.
One-size governance slows everythingWhen a meeting-summary tool gets the same review as a regulated-process tool, teams route around governance entirely.
Leaders see activity, not valuePilots launched and users trained are easy to report. Whether any of it moved a business metric usually isn't.
Regulated risk found lateIn regulated industries like life sciences, discovering a validation or privacy requirement at the pilot stage costs months.

How a use case moves

Every example below uses one fictional sample: an AI transformation office at a life sciences company, with use cases across the three teams a transformation office typically runs (Transformers, Workforce Architects, and AI & Data Innovators). The company and use cases are invented; the governance methods are the ones I use.

1. Use-case intake

One short form for every idea. It's deliberately light, because its main job is to force the two questions most AI proposals skip: what business metric will this move, and how will we know?

FieldWhat it capturesWhy it matters
Problem and ownerThe business problem in one sentence; the accountable business ownerNo owner, no use case
Target metric and baselineThe metric it should move, its current value, and how it's measuredWithout a baseline, value can't be proven later
Users and decisionsWho uses the output, and what decision or action it informsSets the human-oversight design
DataData sources, sensitivity (personal, health, confidential), where it livesDrives privacy and security review
Process contextWhether it touches a regulated process (for example GxP), patients, or external communicationMain input to the risk tier
AutonomyRecommend only, act with approval, or act on its ownHigher autonomy raises the tier
Duplication checkSimilar use cases or tools already in the portfolioReuse before new build

2. Risk tiers

The tier decides how much governance a use case gets. That proportionality is what keeps teams inside the process.

TierTypical profileRequired controlsApprover
Tier 1 · LowInternal productivity; no sensitive data; a person uses the output; easy to reverseIntake record; acceptable-use check; adoption metricWorkstream lead (fast lane, target under a week)
Tier 2 · ModerateInforms internal business decisions; confidential data; recommend onlyTier 1, plus risk assessment, model card, evaluation set with pass thresholds, human-in-the-loop designAI governance forum
Tier 3 · HighTouches patients, regulated processes or submissions, external communications, or health data; or acts without human approvalTier 2, plus validation appropriate to the regulated process, privacy and legal review, failure-mode testing, monitoring plan, named accountable ownerSteering committee with quality and regulatory

4. Stage gates: Go, Iterate, or Stop

Each gate ends in one of three decisions, recorded with the reason. Stopping a use case that isn't working is a governance success, not a failure.

GateQuestionEvidence required
1. Approve to buildIs this worth building, and do we know how we'll measure it?Complete intake; tier assigned; target metric with baseline plan; owner
2. Pilot decisionDoes it work well enough, safely enough?Evaluation results against thresholds; tier controls in place; model card; pilot user feedback
3. ScaleDid the pilot move the metric?Value evidence against baseline; adoption; monitoring and support handoff
Value reviewIs it still delivering, 90 days on?Scorecard actuals; drift or incident review; continue, change, or retire

Go moves to the next stage. Iterate returns to build with specific conditions and a date. Stop retires the use case and records what was learned.

5. Model card

A one-page record that travels with the use case. It answers what a reviewer, a new team member or an auditor will ask first.

  • Purpose and intended users: what it's for, and who should use it
  • Out of scope: uses it must not be put to
  • Model and data: model or platform, data sources, and data that is deliberately excluded
  • Human oversight: where a person reviews, approves, or can override
  • Evaluation: test set, thresholds, and results at the last gate
  • Known limitations: where it fails or is unreliable
  • Monitoring: what's watched in production, and who owns it

Worked example: my Sev1 incident agent

To test the toolkit, I ran a real system through it after the fact: the Copilot Studio agent I built for Severity 1 incident response.

Real system · governance package applied retroactively
RecordResult
IntakeProblem: incident commanders started every Sev1 from zero. Metric: Sev1 time to resolve. Users: incident commanders. Data: status emails, the incident Teams chat, and monitoring data pasted in by hand.
TierTier 2 Internal operations, recommend only, two human gates, and no path to patient data by design.
Controls in placeHuman approval before any action; data boundary through network isolation; approved platform; prompts built on the IT director's standard Sev1 questions.
Controls missingEvaluation set with pass thresholds; structured feedback capture; a plan to measure the agent's contribution separately.
Value scorecardSev1 time to resolve fell 15% in three months, alongside better monitoring and Datadog's Bits AI. Attribution: combined result; the agent's share wasn't isolated.

What the toolkit would have changed: Gate 1 would have required an attribution plan, so the agent's own contribution could be measured. Gate 2 would have required a small evaluation set from past incidents before go-live, which would likely have surfaced the agent's early overconfidence before the team relied on it in live incidents.
